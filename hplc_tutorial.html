Mobile Phase (Solvents)

This is the liquid that carries your sample through the system. In "Reversed-Phase" HPLC (the most common type), we use a mix of:

  • Solvent A (Weak): Purified Water
  • Solvent B (Strong): Acetonitrile or Methanol
💡 Pro-Tip: Degassing

The video shows **degassing** (bubbling helium *through* the solvent). This removes dissolved air, which can cause bubbles in the pump and create a noisy, unstable baseline. Always use degassed solvents!

Instrument Screen

This is your command center. From here, you (or the computer software) will:

  • Set up the entire method (flow rate, gradient, etc.).
  • Monitor the run in real-time.
  • View the live chromatogram as it develops.
  • Check critical system stats like **Pressure** and **Temperature**.
💡 Pro-Tip: The Pressure Reading

Pressure is the HPLC's "heartbeat." A stable pressure (e.g., 2500 psi) is good. A pressure of 0 means the pump is off. A pressure that is climbing too high means you have a **clog**!

Pump & Autosampler

The Pump is the "heart." It's a high-precision machine that mixes Solvents A and B to your exact recipe (the gradient) and pushes them at a constant, high pressure.

The Autosampler is the "robot." The video shows it in action. It precisely draws a tiny amount of your sample from a vial and injects it into the high-pressure liquid stream to start the analysis.

🎯 Parameter Control:

In the simulator, this is where you control the **Flow Rate** (e.g., 1.0 mL/min) and the **Gradient Program** (the %B recipe).

Column Compartment

This is the "brain" of the HPLC. The stainless steel **Column** inside is where the separation actually happens. It's packed with a solid material (like C18) that interacts with your sample.

The Oven** holds the column at a stable temperature (e.g., 30°C). This is critical because temperature changes can (and will) change your peak retention times, ruining your analysis.

🎯 Parameter Control:

In the simulator, this is where you control the **Column Temperature**. A higher temperature often makes peaks elute *faster* and become *sharper*.

Problem: Tailing Peaks

Your peaks look like "shark fins." They rise quickly but have a long, slow tail on the right. This is bad because it's hard for the software to tell where the peak ends, and it can hide small impurity peaks.

💡 Common Solutions:

1. **Check pH:** The most common cause. Your mobile phase pH might be wrong for your compound.
2. **Lower Sample Load:** You injected *too much* sample. Try diluting it.
3. **Increase Temperature:** A higher column temp can sometimes sharpen peaks.

Problem: Poor Resolution

Your peaks are "mashed" or "fused" together. The valley between them doesn't return to the baseline. You can't accurately measure the area of either peak.

💡 Common Solutions:

1. **Slow Down the Gradient:** This is the best fix. Make your gradient ramp *slower* (e.g., from 8 minutes to 12 minutes). This gives the peaks more time to separate.
2. **Change Solvent B:** Maybe Acetonitrile isn't the best. Try Methanol (or vice-versa).
3. **Lower the Flow Rate:** Slower flow (e.g., 0.8 mL/min) can increase separation.

Problem: Noisy Baseline

Your baseline is fuzzy, jagged, or has "static." This makes it impossible to detect very small impurity peaks and throws off your calculations.

💡 Common Solutions:

1. **Degas Solvents:** This is the #1 cause. Dissolved air is getting into the pump. Re-degas your solvents (like in the video!).
2. **Check for Leaks:** A tiny, loose fitting can draw in air. Check all connections.
3. **Failing Lamp:** The detector's lamp might be old and dying.

Problem: No Peaks At All!

You ran your sample, the time ticked by, and... nothing. A flat line. This is a very common (and scary) problem!

💡 Common Solutions:

1. **Check the Autosampler:** Did the needle actually go into the vial? Is the vial empty? Did the injection fail?
2. **Check the Detector:** Are you at the *wrong wavelength*? If your compound absorbs at 254nm and your detector is set to 210nm, you won't see it.
3. **Check the Sample:** Is your sample diluted *too much*? Is it the wrong sample?
