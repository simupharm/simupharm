Instrument Control

This is your command center. From the software, you control every aspect of the analysis:

  • Background Scan: You MUST run this first! It measures the air (CO₂ and H₂O) to subtract it from your sample.
  • Sample Scan: Runs the actual analysis on your material.
  • Parameters: You set the **Resolution** (peak sharpness) and **Number of Scans** (higher = less noise).
💡 Pro-Tip: Purging

Real labs "purge" the instrument with dry nitrogen gas to remove all air. A bad purge (or no purge) is the #1 cause of messy spectra. Always run a fresh background scan!

ATR Accessory

This is the **Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR)** module. It makes testing solids and liquids incredibly easy. It replaces the old, difficult method of making KBr pellets.

The IR beam is directed *into* the small crystal (the shiny circle). The sample is pressed on top, and the beam interacts with it at the surface. This is the most common accessory in a modern pharma lab.

Sample Stage & Press

This is where the magic happens. A tiny amount of your sample (powder or liquid) is placed directly onto the ATR crystal.

The **Pressure Arm** is then lowered and tightened. This is critical! It ensures good, uniform contact between the sample and the crystal. Bad contact is a major source of bad data.

🎯 The "Golden Rule" of ATR:

If your peaks look weak and your baseline is wavy, your first step is to **re-apply the pressure** and run the scan again. 9 times out of 10, this fixes it.

Problem: Messy Spectrum (Air)

Your spectrum has sharp, spiky "devil horns" around **2350 cm⁻¹** and a broad, messy "lump" of peaks from **3600-3000 cm⁻¹**.

💡 Diagnosis & Solution:

This is classic **atmospheric interference**. The "horns" are **CO₂** and the lump is **H₂O (water vapor)** from the air.
Solution: You ran your *sample* without running a *background* scan first! Go back, click **"Acquire New" (Background)**, wait for it to finish, and *then* run your sample. This will subtract the air perfectly.

Problem: Negative Peaks

Your spectrum looks strange, with sharp peaks going *down* (negative) where the CO₂ and water peaks should be.

💡 Diagnosis & Solution:

This happens when your **background scan is "dirtier" than your sample scan**. For example, you ran a background when the air was full of CO₂, then the instrument purged, and *then* you ran your sample.
Solution: The background scan is no longer valid. You must **run a new background scan** and then immediately re-run your sample.

Problem: Slanted Baseline

Your peaks look weak, and the entire baseline is on a steep slant or a broad "hump." It doesn't sit flat at zero.

💡 Diagnosis & Solution:

This is almost always a **sample prep problem**.
Cause 1 (Most Likely): Bad ATR contact. The sample isn't pressed down hard enough or evenly.
Cause 2:** You are using a "bad" KBr pellet that was too thick or cracked.
Solution: Re-apply your sample to the ATR crystal, ensure the pressure arm is tight, and run the scan again. You can also use the **"Baseline Correct"** tool in the software to fix this mathematically.

Problem: Extra Peaks!

You see your correct sample peaks (e.g., Polystyrene), but there are also three *huge*, "greasy" peaks between **2950-2800 cm⁻¹** that don't belong.

💡 Diagnosis & Solution:

This is a **classic contaminant!** Those are the C-H stretch peaks from **Nujol (mineral oil)**. This spectrum was prepared using a "Nujol Mull" instead of ATR.
Solution: As an analyst, you must learn to **mentally ignore** these contaminant peaks. You know they are from your prep, so you focus on the *other* peaks (the "fingerprint region" < 1500 cm⁻¹) to identify your sample.
